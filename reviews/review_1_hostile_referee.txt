# Bottom line

I treated this like a hostile referee + reproducibility auditor + ML reviewer + detector-simulation reviewer.

As a **rough internal validation report**, this is already unusually disciplined: it separates structural guarantees from physics fidelity, reports bad results instead of hiding them, distinguishes evaluator seeds from training seeds, explicitly refuses to claim speedup, and documents the contaminated-test issue rather than burying it. 

As a **publishable fast-simulation paper**, though, it is not there yet. The biggest problems are not prose. They are scientific-definition and experimental-design problems:

**The five things I would stop and resolve before spending much time polishing text are:** the possible incompleteness of the conditioning variables; whether the current Gaussian-mixture response head actually guarantees positive response; validation/test reuse and contamination; whether the loss/architecture screening protocol is statistically meaningful; and the fact that the manuscript currently lacks enough detector/Geant4/model detail to reproduce the result.

There is also one potentially important mathematical issue in the “common-measure” correction, one potentially important mismatch between the support-training loss and Gumbel-Top-k sampling law, and one very revealing objective-function failure in S2 that I think deserves much more emphasis.

# Audit pass 1 — what the paper actually claims

The manuscript is strongest when it says, essentially, “we have built a structurally valid generator, but it is not yet a good simulator.” That distinction is explicit in the abstract, the model section, and the conclusion.  

But the **title still says “Fast Monte Carlo Generator.”** You have no end-to-end timing yet, and the manuscript explicitly says speedup is not established. That makes “Fast” an unproved property in the title even though the body is careful. For an internal draft this is fine; for submission, either timing has to arrive or the title should become something like “hierarchical sparse conditional generator” until speed is demonstrated.

The abstract phrase **“a recent selected checkpoint”** is also weaker than the rest of the manuscript. “Recent” is time-relative and “selected” immediately raises “selected how?” Name the checkpoint and its selection rule. Right now the reader later has to infer that you mean V3-SUP.

The phrase **“these results support a documented, falsifiable research candidate”** is unusual scientific language. The results demonstrate execution and identify failures. They do not really “support” the candidate in the normal evidentiary sense.

# Audit pass 2 — data governance is currently the largest experimental-design issue

The manuscript says the canonical split is 612,482 / 76,158 / 76,300, but the actual optimization lineage uses only 26,624 training and 6,656 pilot-validation events. 

That means the current model was trained on only about **4.35% of the canonical training set**. Consequently, essentially every negative model-quality conclusion needs the qualifier that this may be a pilot-data or optimization limitation rather than an architectural ceiling. The manuscript does say “pilot,” but a reader could still give too much weight to the detailed failure analysis.

The test history is much more serious. You disclose that 40,000 test events were used in an external classifier experiment and another 200 in a visual draw, with unresolved overlap. 

That is admirably transparent, but this sentence is too strong:

> “Neither earlier use informed model choices.”

If people developing the model saw those results, it is difficult to establish that they had literally no influence. A safer statement would eventually describe **what was done with them**, who saw them, and whether any recorded architecture/hyperparameter decision followed them.

More importantly, if the identities of the used events are genuinely unresolved, then you cannot currently identify a demonstrably untouched exact subset of the nominal test set. “Between 36,100 and 36,300 have never been used” is arithmetically true, but scientifically useless until you know **which** ones.

There is a second governance issue that the manuscript does not confront strongly enough: the same 10,000-condition “validation battery” is being repeatedly inspected while architectures and screens are developed. 

Once S1/S2/S3/etc. decisions are made using that bank, it is really a **development set**, not an unbiased final validation set. Freezing the conditions does not prevent adaptive overfitting to the fixed evaluation bank.

You have enough validation events to solve this cleanly. I would eventually separate “architecture-development validation” from a genuinely locked “final validation” before touching the test set.

# Audit pass 3 — I found a potentially serious positivity contradiction

This is the first thing I would inspect in code.

You state that for visible events the original response head models

`log(1 + T/s_T)`

with a conditional Gaussian mixture. 

But an ordinary Gaussian mixture has support on the entire real line. If

`z = log(1 + T/s_T)`

then inversion gives

`T = s_T (exp(z) - 1)`.

For **z < 0**, this produces **T < 0**.

Yet the abstract and decoder discussion assert finite nonnegative outputs and nonnegative energy budgets.  

So one of four things must be happening but is not stated: you truncate the Gaussian mixture; clamp the transformed sample; transform the mixture output through another positive map; or the claimed nonnegativity is not actually mathematically guaranteed.

This is not a stylistic omission. Depending on the implementation, it could invalidate one of the paper's main structural claims.

A related terminology problem occurs when you say the flow “produces logits for nonnegative shares \(q_\ell\), whose masked softmax gives” the layer budgets. 

**Logits are not nonnegative shares.** The softmax outputs are the nonnegative shares. Either \(q_\ell\) are logits or \(q_\ell\) are shares; the present wording conflates the two.

# Audit pass 4 — the flow/share mathematics is drastically under-specified

The flow-matching paragraph gives the generic interpolation and velocity target, but not enough information to reproduce either the profile or cell-share flow. 

The important missing part is the **data coordinate system**.

Layer shares live on a simplex. Cell shares live on a variable-dimensional simplex. Yet your flow is written as Euclidean interpolation between \(x_0\sim N(0,I)\) and transformed data \(x_1\). What exactly is \(x_1\)?

Is it log-share? Additive-log-ratio coordinates? Centered-log-ratio? Raw logits obtained by an inverse softmax with a gauge choice? Something else?

The sentence “for a data transform \(x_1\)” acknowledges a transform exists, but the paper never defines it. That transform is central to the actual model.

There is also an ancestral-training question: at training time, does the cell-share flow receive **ground-truth support \(H\)** and ground-truth layer budget \(D\), or support/budget sampled by the upstream model? If it is teacher-forced on truth and sampled hierarchically only at inference, exposure error can accumulate badly through response → activity → count → support → share. Given the large topology failure, this matters a lot.

# Audit pass 5 — Gumbel-Top-k needs more careful probabilistic language

The manuscript says that exactly \(K_\ell\) channels are selected by one Gumbel-Top-k sample. 

That procedure is principled: Gumbel-Top-k produces sampling without replacement from the score-induced categorical/Plackett-Luce structure. ([Proceedings of Machine Learning Research][1])

But your **training losses are support BCE plus support ranking loss**, not an explicitly described likelihood for the Plackett-Luce subset distribution. 

That does not automatically make the method wrong. It does mean the paper should not leave the impression that the support-stage training objective is the likelihood corresponding to the Gumbel-Top-k generative law.

The exact relationship between the trained channel scores and the sampled unordered \(K\)-subset needs to be made explicit.

# Audit pass 6 — the common-measure correction deserves a derivation

This line caught my attention:

> “The audited common-measure total-loss offset for historical runs is +0.421936354321.”



For

`z = log(1 + T/s_T)`,

the density transformation is

`p_T(T) = p_z(z) |dz/dT| = p_z(z)/(s_T + T)`,

so

`NLL_T = NLL_z + log(s_T + T)`,

up to whatever convention/units you are using.

That correction is **sample-dependent**. Its mean on a fixed validation dataset can indeed be a constant, but then \(0.421936354321\) is an **empirical mean Jacobian correction on a particular set under a particular averaging convention**, not a universal transformation constant.

If the number is additionally multiplied by a response-loss weight, averaged only over visible events, or normalized in some other way, that also needs to be shown.

Right now I cannot audit whether this correction is mathematically correct from the manuscript.

# Audit pass 7 — S2 reveals that your training objective may be badly misaligned

This is potentially one of the most important scientific observations in the entire draft.

S2 improves the reported common-measure validation loss from 4.935508 to 4.921213, yet the generated zero fraction goes from approximately 1% to **50.41%**, the high-level C2ST AUC gets dramatically worse, and the mean active-layer count collapses from about 54 to 25.5. 

The manuscript correctly says that aggregate loss cannot override physics diagnostics. But this deserves a harder conclusion:

**Your current scalar training/selection objective is capable of preferring a manifestly catastrophic generative distribution.**

Before the final experiment, I would want a full per-component loss decomposition for M0 and S2. In particular, I want to know how a model generating zero responses for half its events does not receive enough visibility-BCE penalty to dominate a 0.014 improvement elsewhere.

There may be a perfectly mundane explanation—loss weighting, stochastic sample fraction versus calibrated probabilities, joint encoder movement—but that explanation has to be shown.

This also argues for **promotion gates**, not just scalar-loss ranking: zero-mass fidelity, response-by-energy-bin, activity structure, C2ST, etc.

# Audit pass 8 — C2ST is currently too opaque to be a central result

C2ST is one of your main evaluation results, but I still do not know the evaluator architecture, feature lists, internal train/evaluation split, sample sizes, preprocessing, hyperparameters, whether conditions are included, or how the three evaluator seeds differ. 

The sentence about “high-level,” “low-level,” and “profile-aware” feature families is not sufficient.

The frozen **0.65 AUROC threshold** also needs a justification. AUC is naturally interpreted relative to 0.5; 0.65 is an operational criterion you invented, not a statistically privileged boundary. The manuscript correctly says the scores are not significance tests, but plotting the red threshold gives it more authority than the text warrants. 

The condition-only classifier result of 0.4636 is probably compatible with chance, but you should report uncertainty. More generally, classifier optimizer-seed variation is not the same thing as **sampling uncertainty in the estimated AUC**.

And if the classifier is strong enough to give 0.78–0.93 AUC, that is good evidence of distinguishability. Conversely, if it eventually gets 0.51, that would **not** prove distributional equivalence; it could reflect an underpowered evaluator. Your language mostly respects that distinction already.

computed only among \(T>0\) events separately for generator and Geant4?

# Audit pass 17 — what I think a hostile reviewer would attack first

A detector reviewer will ask whether your conditioning variables actually specify the shower geometry and why there is no Geant4 configuration.

An ML reviewer will ask what the actual architecture is, what coordinate transform your flows use, why the scalar loss rewards S2, whether repeated validation-bank inspection invalidates model-selection claims, and whether support-BCE training corresponds to the Gumbel-Top-k sampling law.

A statistics reviewer will ask for uncertainty, repeated conditional samples, multiple training seeds, evaluator holdouts, and justification for the 0.65 threshold.

A fast-simulation reviewer will say there is no speed benchmark, no competent baseline, and no reconstruction-level evidence.

A reproducibility reviewer will like the hashes but ask why none of the exact configuration/report artifacts appears publicly accessible yet.

A skeptical editor may simply say: **this is a transparent progress report describing an unfinished simulator; where is the completed scientific result?**

That is exactly why I would not spend much effort beautifying prose yet. The paper itself already admits essentially the same thing in its limitations and conclusion.
